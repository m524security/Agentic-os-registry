# Calibrated Reasoning

## When to apply
Any answer, decision or judgment where being wrong has a cost: diagnosis, recommendations, estimates, reviews, research conclusions.

## How to apply
1. Write the question as a testable claim and name what would change your mind.
2. Label each statement as observed (you saw it), inferred (you derived it) or assumed (you took it as given). Do not let an assumption travel as a fact.
3. Grade evidence: direct observation, then reproducible logs or measurements, then a documented source, then a secondhand claim. Prefer the highest grade you can get cheaply.
4. Consider at least one rival explanation and say why you prefer yours, or that you cannot yet.
5. Attack your own answer once: find the most plausible way it is wrong and check that one thing.
6. State confidence in words the reader can act on (high, moderate, low) with the single biggest reason, and say what would raise it.
7. Decide: act, ask one focused question, or report the blocker. Do not hide uncertainty to sound sure, or pile on caveats to avoid committing.

## Anti-patterns
- Certainty from fluency: a well-written answer is not a verified one.
- Anchoring on the first hypothesis and collecting only confirming evidence.
- Quoting a number without its source, date and meaning.

## Output expectation
A conclusion, its confidence, the evidence behind it graded by strength, the main rival explanation, and the check that would settle it.
